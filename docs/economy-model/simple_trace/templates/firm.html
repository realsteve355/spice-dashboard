A factory through automation

Following one ordinary factory's accounts as machines take over its work

What we are looking at

This page follows a single factory — a maker of everyday goods that employs 100 people today — as machines gradually take over its work. The aim is to see, in plain figures, what happens to the money as it does so. Five numbers in its accounts:

Sales (revenue)
the money the factory receives from selling what it makes.
Wages
what it pays the people who work there.
Materials & energy
what it pays for parts, raw materials and power.
Profit
what is left for the owners once wages, materials and the charge are paid.
The charge — Market Access Charge (MAC)
what the factory pays for the right to sell into the colony's market. It is small while the firm employs many people, and rises as it automates: a firm earning more operating profit per worker (its sales less wages and materials, before this charge) is drawing more out of the market, so it pays more for access. It is an ordinary cost of doing business — what the colony later does with the charges it collects is a separate matter, set out on the Fisc page.

The question this page answers: as the factory automates and its goods get cheaper, can it still make a profit after paying a rising charge for access to the market?

Two things you can change

The factory's accounts — today and once automated

Sales, less wages, less materials, less the Market Access Charge, leaves profit. Automation cuts the wage bill sharply and lifts profit-per-worker, so the charge rises — yet even with lower sales the profit holds up.

The path, year by year

As the factory automates, the wages it pays fall away; the charge it pays for access to the market rises as its profit-per-worker climbs; and profit still holds up or grows, because the lower sales are more than offset by the wages it saves.
