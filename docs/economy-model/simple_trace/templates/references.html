Daron Acemoglu (the principal sceptic)

Institute Professor, MIT · 2024 Nobel Prize in Economics
"Macroeconomic effects appear nontrivial but modest — no more than a 0.66% increase in total factor productivity over 10 years."
"The Simple Macroeconomics of AI", NBER w32487, April 2024. Methodologically grounded in Hulten's theorem applied to actual task-level cost savings. Direct counter to McKinsey/Goldman.
NBER w32487, 2024 · MIT preprint
"Only 20% of US labour tasks are exposed to AI; even fewer can be profitably automated."
Foundational claim — exposure ≠ automation. Bulls implicitly assume most exposed tasks get automated; Acemoglu shows the second filter is very restrictive.
NBER w32487 §3
Three places AI productivity gains can flow: capital owners (margins), consumers (lower prices), or labour (new tasks).
Acemoglu-Restrepo task-based model — the framework underlying SPICE's profitability scenarios. Shows that the distributional outcome is contingent, not predetermined.
Acemoglu-Restrepo, Econometrica 2022
Roughly half of the decline in labor's share since the 1980s stemmed from automation and technological change.
Pascual Restrepo (Yale) — empirical decomposition. Sets the historical base rate for what current AI may continue.
Economy.ac — Corporate Profits Surge While Labor's Share Shrinks

McKinsey Global Institute

Consulting research arm
"Generative AI has the potential to generate value equivalent to $2.6 trillion to $4.4 trillion in global corporate profits annually."
"The Economic Potential of Generative AI" report, June 2023. The headline number for AI's profit upside.
McKinsey MGI, June 2023
"Generative AI could increase labor productivity by 0.1% to 0.6% annually through 2040, plus 0.2 to 3.3 ppt from broader automation."
Wide-range estimate — sits between Acemoglu's 0.05%/yr and the most optimistic accelerationist scenarios.
McKinsey MGI, 2023

Theoretical ceiling. Note "theoretically" — Acemoglu's filter (profitable to automate) cuts this dramatically.
McKinsey MGI, 2023

BLS — US Bureau of Labor Statistics

Official US price-index data
Computer prices declined 16%/yr 1990–2005; 11%/yr 2001–2005 (with hedonic quality adjustment).
The empirical foundation for the bull thesis. Sustained, multi-decade tech-goods deflation already happened.
BLS — Long-term Price Trends for Computers
Apparel CPI: averaged +1.98%/yr historically; recently DEFLATING at −0.5%/yr (early 2026).
Clothing has crossed from inflation to deflation in the US. Manufactured goods generally on the same trajectory.
BLS CPI Home
Housing CPI averaged +4.23%/yr historically; Medical care +4.56%/yr.
The two stickiest-inflation categories — both have land/labour-intensive cost bases that resist deflation.
BLS Historical CPI

Knoll, Schularick & Steger — "No Price Like Home: Global House Prices, 1870–2012"

American Economic Review, Feb 2017
"Real house prices stayed constant from the nineteenth to the mid-twentieth century, but rose strongly and with substantial cross-country variation in the second half of the twentieth century."
140 years of data, 14 advanced economies. The canonical long-run land-price dataset.
AER, 2017
"Rising land prices explain about 80% of the global house price boom that has taken place since World War II."
CRITICAL for SPICE — empirically establishes that LAND, not structures, drives housing cost growth. Justifies separating the two in the basket model.
Same paper, headline finding

Wright's Law / Experience Curves

1936 aerospace observation, validated across many industries since
Solar PV: 20% price decline per doubling of cumulative production, sustained for 40+ years.
The cleanest example of Wright's Law at scale. Underpins the Energy category's deflation rate in the basket.
Our World in Data — Learning Curves
Lithium-ion batteries: 18%/doubling historically; recent doublings closer to 35%/doubling.
Storage cost decline is what makes intermittent renewables match dispatchable fossil. Accelerating.
Our World in Data
Semiconductors: 20–30% price decline per doubling (BCG 1966 origin of the "experience curve" term).
Original case. Drives Moore's Law and AI compute cost.
Wikipedia — Experience curve effect
