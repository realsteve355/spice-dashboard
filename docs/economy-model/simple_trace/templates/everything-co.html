Everything & Co

One company makes the whole basket. As it automates, can a charge on it fund a basic income — and what happens to the price?

The set-up

Imagine a single company — Everything & Co — that makes the whole basket of essentials for a town of 1,000 working-age people, one basket each. Today a basket costs $20,000, and the company makes a 15% profit on it.

Over 20 years it automates: the cost of making a basket falls from 100% to 15% of today. As it does, more and more people are out of work — from 4% to 75% — and each of them needs a basic income large enough to buy a basket. That basic income is funded by a charge on Everything & Co (the Market Access Charge).

The catch: a charge can only be paid out of a surplus, and a surplus only exists if the basket's price stays above the cost of making it. So the price can't simply fall to the cheaper cost — it has to stay high enough to raise the basic income for everyone now out of work. This page solves, year by year, for the price where the charge exactly funds the basic income — and shows the company's own profit getting squeezed as it does.

Year by year

The charge each year equals the basic-income bill (people out of work × the basket price). "E&Co keeps" is its gross profit after paying the charge — what's left for the owners.
