Funding a basic income when robots do the work

The honest version — the MAC recovers most of it, the rest is issued money that automation's falling costs absorb. No closed-loop magic.

The idea, in plain terms (the honest version)

Robots do all the work, so no one earns a wage. The only way people can buy anything is a basic income — the community treasury (the Fisc) issues everyone a set amount each month.

The robots make the essentials nearly for free. So think of money as the ration coupon that lets people claim those goods. The basic income issues the coupons; the Fisc takes a cut of each automated sale — the Market Access Charge (MAC) — and pulls most of the coupons back, so the same money can be issued again next month.

It does not all come back, and we don't pretend it does. The automated firms keep a slim profit (which still beats today, because they pay no wages), and the human-craft shops aren't charged at all. The part the MAC can't reach is simply issued as new money — and that stays non-inflationary because automation is making everything cheaper at the same time. The deflation is the headroom. Where the money goes — for every sale

Each sale is split: the Fisc takes the MAC, the robots cost a little to run, and the firm keeps the rest. The human shops pay no MAC — that money is a person's wage for real work.

Why a firm might accept it

Even handing the Fisc a big cut of every sale, an automated firm can keep a margin above today's — because it pays no wages. But only up to a point: push the MAC too high and the margin drops below today's. Watch this table and the issued-money figure move against each other as you change the rates.
