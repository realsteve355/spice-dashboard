The central mechanism — a targeted Market Access Charge

Each company pays a rate that rises with its profit-per-employee. Heavily automated firms (high profit, few employees) pay a large share of profit; labour-intensive and human-centric businesses pay very little. This protects human work while funding UBI.

How company profits evolve as automation advances

Growth rates are derived from the input-cost deflation on the Cost Deflation page: automatable cost bases collapse (manufactured goods −11%/yr, transport −12%/yr, energy −15%/yr, compute −21%/yr), expanding the margins of automated producers — logistics, manufacturing, large-scale retail, platforms. Human-centric and craft businesses — cafés, trades, care, bespoke makers — are labour-bound (their value is the human element, and labour does not deflate) and face rising land costs, so their profits stay flat to squeezed. Each line is the real-profit index (2026 = 100): profits rise fastest while input costs are collapsing (2026–2035), then flatten as costs bottom out — the gains are front-loaded, not compounding. The 2046 multiple is in the legend.

What this means

Profits diverge

Over twenty years the automated firms' profits expand roughly three- to five-fold — fastest in the first decade as input costs collapse, then flattening. Human-centric businesses barely move, and the most labour-bound are squeezed by rising land. The profit pool tilts steadily toward the most automated companies — the same firms that already sit at the high end of the charge.

Why it matters for the charge

The targeted charge falls hardest on high-profit-per-employee firms — and those are exactly the firms whose profits grow. So the county MAC income rises over time even with the rate structure fixed: a growing share of automation's gains is channelled back into the colony, precisely as human work is displaced.
