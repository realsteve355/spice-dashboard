Best configurations — sweep results

The simulator was run 38 times with different parameter combinations under The Transition (and four under each scenario for cross-validation). This page summarises what the sweep found. The headline: steady ground is achievable, but only via deliberate policy choices that go beyond mild tweaks. Three configurations hit full steady ground under Transition; another two reach strong defence; the realistic-feeling middle ground lands in partial defence.

1. Headline finding

Under The Transition (the credibility test) at 10% scale, 120 months:

FULL STEADY GROUND3 configurations achieve PP loss = 0% with peg held throughout
STRONG DEFENCE2 more configurations land below 17% PP loss
PARTIAL DEFENCE3-4 configurations in the 25-35% range
WEAK DEFENCE~12 configurations between weak-defence and worse-than-USD
WORSE THAN USD~14 configurations including the unmitigated baseline (66.8% loss)

The three full steady ground configurations all involve a structural restriction on flows, not just parameter tuning:

  • Capital controls (cashout multiplier 0): citizens cannot convert S → USDC. Peg holds throughout. Politically heavy: it's a hard ban on individual capital flight.
  • Means-tested UBI (retirees-only) + MPC 3%: only retirees + ubi-only citizens get UBI. Cuts ~70% of UBI mint. Mechanically perfect but changes the SPICE design from universal-basic to safety-net.
  • Maximum mitigation stack (MPC 15% + both refinancings + S-tax 5%): no single lever extreme but everything turned up. Politically least disruptive of the three; just heavy on intervention.

The big movers are flow restrictions, not flow taxes. Stopping outflows (capital controls, refinancings) does much more than taxing internal flows (S-tax) or capacity (MPC). The intuition: under Transition, the colony's reserve drains because of large structural USDC outflows (mortgages, cashouts). MPC and S-tax burn S to slow supply growth — useful but secondary, because the reserve is what backs the peg, not a low S supply per se.

5. Counter-intuitive findings

Higher UBI makes things much worse

UBI 150 → 77.7% PP loss (vs baseline 66.8%). UBI 50 → 38.6% PP loss. The cashflow sequence: more UBI minted = more S in citizen wallets = more cashouts = more reserve drain. Above some threshold the marginal increase in citizens' nominal income is fully eaten by faster S devaluation. Welfare-first policy backfires under Transition without strong reserve defence.

Cover ratio 0.40 (more conservative) is WORSE than 0.30

Counter-intuitive: a fatter reserve buffer should be safer. But forcing cover ratio higher means the Fisc compresses the rate sooner, accelerating the death-spiral. Cover 0.40 → peg breaks M40 (Y3.3) vs baseline M47 (Y4). Cover 0.15 (looser) actually helps slightly. Implication: defending too aggressively early is worse than defending later — the reserve compounds with itself in a way that means earlier compression bites worse.

S-tax is essentially useless

Even 5% S-tax on every internal purchase only shaves 0.5pp off the baseline PP loss. The reason: internal purchase volume is much smaller than expected — at 10% scale, ~120K S/month of total basket spending, vs ~390K S/month of UBI minting. S-tax burns at most ~6K S/month, marginal against the supply growth.

External rent refi alone is also marginal

Only 208 external-rent households at 10% scale (vs 746 mortgage households). Refinancing them saves ~$3.7M/yr USDC outflow vs ~$13M/yr from mortgages. ~3pp improvement — meaningful but small relative to mortgage refi.

Means-tested UBI is mechanically the strongest individual policy

Cutting UBI to retirees + ubi_only_choice citizens (about 30% of the population) eliminates ~70% of the UBI mint. Peg holds. But this fundamentally changes the SPICE design — it's no longer "universal" basic income. Politically very different. Worth knowing as the upper-bound mechanical lever, not a recommendation.

"Capital controls" wins by being the cleanest

Cashout multiplier = 0 means citizens cannot convert S to USDC. The peg holds without needing MPC, refinancing, or anything else. Implementation in real terms could be: a founding-period rule (no cashout for first N years) that relaxes once reserve is built up, OR a tiered cashout limit (citizens can convert N S/month, no more). This is a serious policy choice but the simulator suggests it might be the most efficient path to steady ground.

6. Recommended configurations

If accepting capital controls is acceptable: Founding-period restriction

Best mechanical solution: cashout = 0 for first 5 years, relaxing afterwards. Single lever, perfect peg defence, doesn't require ongoing intervention. Real-world parallel: emerging-market currency unions often have founding-period capital controls that relax with credibility. Caveat: we tested permanent cashout=0; a relaxing-after-Y5 variant should be tested before recommending.

If "universal" UBI is non-negotiable: Maximum mitigation stack

MPC 15% + mortgage refi + external rent refi + S-tax 5%. Achieves full steady ground via heavy intervention without restricting capital flows or means-testing. Politically defensible: it's "make automation pay tax + colony bank buyout of household debt + transaction tax" — none of these is constitutionally novel. Cost: ongoing tax + admin burden.

If acceptable to means-test UBI for working-age citizens: Means-tested + light MPC

Retirees-only UBI + MPC 3% achieves perfect peg defence. Different SPICE — basic income as elderly pension + automation-funded safety net for the vulnerable. Working-age citizens earn entirely via dividends + external income. Politically loaded. This is a different design, not a tweaked version of the original.

The realistic-feeling middle: MPC 5% + both refinancings

Partial defence under Transition (29% PP loss), full steady ground under all milder scenarios. The "reasonable mitigations" tier — defensible in a real political conversation, doesn't restrict individual capital flows or change SPICE's universal-UBI character. Citizens lose ~30% real purchasing power if Transition hits hard, but the colony still beats USD-only. Worth running by default if no other constraint dominates.

7. What's still open

  • Phase 3 effects. Not in this sweep: company founding by strivers, company failures, births/deaths. Phase 3 might shift the picture if new exporters emerge during the simulation, providing additional reserve replenishment.
  • Time-varying restrictions. All sweep configs use static parameters across the 10-year horizon. A "cashout 0 for years 0-3, then 0.5×, then full" trajectory would be cleaner than permanent capital controls and likely produce similar peg defence at lower social cost.
  • Higher cover ratio combined with strong reserve growth. Cover 0.40 hurt because reserve was thin. With a strong reserve-replenishment combo (MPC 10%+) we should test cover 0.40 again.
  • The Joe-the-mortgaged-worker problem. Even with the best mitigations, individual mortgaged citizens are insolvent because mortgage burdens were sized for external US wages. See /families for the lived reality. Founding-data fix needed: shrink mortgage assumptions or make mortgage refi mandatory at colony founding.
  • 20-year horizon. 10 years is the credibility window but real colonies will run for longer. Configurations that work at 10y but fail at 20y aren't really steady ground.
  • Healthcare crisis scenario wasn't included in cross-scenario validation. Worth a follow-up run.

Bottom line. The simulator answers Steve's framing question — "find the configuration that delivers steady ground" — with a yes, but with a price. There is no parameter combination that achieves full steady ground under Transition without either (a) restricting capital flows, (b) means-testing UBI, or (c) running heavy ongoing taxation. The realistic middle ground (MPC 5% + refinancings, no other restrictions) achieves partial defence. Whether partial defence counts as "good enough" is a political question, not a numerical one.
