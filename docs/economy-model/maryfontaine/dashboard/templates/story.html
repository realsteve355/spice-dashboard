The Transition story

Year-by-year, what happens to a 3,900-person colony under the Transition scenario at default settings. UBI 100 S/month, no LAT, no mortgage refinance. Honda Inc owns 60% of the factory but doesn't extract dividends — the value flow is the cars they buy as an export customer, not a profit dividend. The peg breaks in year 3. By year 10, citizens have lost 67% of real purchasing power. Then we look at what happens with mitigations on.

1. The setup

The colony starts with 3,900 citizens across 12 archetypes, 2,200 households, 285 companies. The Fisc has $5M USDC reserve. Total founding S supply is ~$2.5M in company working capital. The basket of goods costs $28 USD = 28 S at parity ($1/S).

The Transition scenario is the credibility test: AI deflation in tradable goods accelerating non-linearly through the decade (-8% Y1 → -38% Y5 → -10% Y10), simultaneous USD inflation in services and energy from monetary response (energy +10% Y1 → +22% Y4 → +2% Y10), and food inflation peaking mid-decade. By Y10, the basket has cost in USD has roughly doubled from $28 to $58, but the composition has shifted dramatically — energy and services up, hard goods nearly worthless.

The Fisc's job: keep the basket costing 28 S throughout, regardless of what the underlying USD prices do. Citizens shouldn't experience the wider world's chaos — they should see stable prices in their own currency.

First 24 months: things look fine. UBI mints 4.7M S/year. Honda exports $4.5M/year, remote workers bring in ~$10M/year, retiree pensions add ~$3.8M/year. Total inflows ~$18M/year. Outflows: imports ~$5M, mortgages ~$13M, external rent ~$3.7M, citizen cashouts ~$1-2M. Net: roughly balanced, slightly positive in early years.

The reserve grows from $5M to $14M as the colony cashes in on Honda exports. The Fisc rate edges up from $1 to $1.26 because basket USD prices are rising (energy inflation outpacing goods deflation in early Transition years). Cover ratio drops from 0.80 to 0.43 — still well above the 0.30 floor.

The colony looks like it's working. This is the dangerous bit — a viewer could see Y1 and Y2 numbers and conclude the design holds. The stress hasn't started compounding yet.

3. Year 3 — the peg breaks

Year-endFisc rateReserveS supplyBasket SCompressed?
Y3 (M48)$1.36$10.9M$27.5M29.55YES

Sometime around month 36, the cover-ratio constraint catches up. S supply has grown to $27.5M. At the basket-anchored target rate of $1.44/S (basket USD = $40.25 at this point), required reserve = $27.5M × $1.44 × 0.30 = $11.9M. But reserve is only $10.9M.

The Fisc has two choices: let cover ratio breach the floor, or force the rate down. It forces the rate down to $1.36 — what the reserve can actually back. At that rate, basket cost in S = $40.25 / $1.36 = 29.55 S.

Citizens now experience inflation in their own currency. Their monthly UBI of 100 S buys 100/29.55 = 3.38 baskets, down from 3.57 at parity. That's a 5% real loss in the first year of compression. The peg has broken; from here it gets worse every month.

4. Years 4–6 — slow grind

Y6 (M84)$0.84$10.4M$41.3M62.80

Once the peg breaks, every month makes it worse. UBI keeps minting 390K S/month unconditionally. Mortgages keep draining USDC every month. Worker citizens keep cashing out a fraction of their growing dividend income. Remote workers keep bringing some USDC in but not enough.

The Fisc is now in a death-spiral feedback: S supply grows → basket cost in S grows → citizens have less purchasing power → fewer cashouts (good for reserve) but also fewer purchases (bad for company revenue) → companies have less S to pay dividends → less S goes to citizens → but UBI still keeps minting.

Critically, the reserve is not depleting catastrophically — it sits in the $9-10M range throughout. The Fisc is doing its job: it's defending the cover ratio at exactly 0.30. What it's giving up is the basket peg.

By Y10, the basket costs 84.46 S. A citizen on UBI alone (100 S/month) buys 1.18 baskets — barely above subsistence. At founding they bought 3.57 baskets. That's a 67% loss in real purchasing power.

Note the reserve has actually recovered slightly to $11.7M by Y10. The Fisc's defence is technically successful — the colony hasn't gone bankrupt. But the cost is paid by the citizens, who absorb the entire shock through devalued S.

6. Joe's trajectory — the citizen view

Joe is a 35-year-old Honda assembly worker. At founding he owns 0.5 time-limited shares in Honda. He lives in a 2-adult, 2-child household. They have a $200K mortgage at 5% with 240 months remaining (~$1,320/month).

Year 0

  • UBI: 100 S × 4 family members = 400 S/month (kids' UBI accrues to household primary)
  • Honda dividend: ~375 S/month (his 0.5 share of distributable surplus, with Honda Inc's slice redistributed to workers + colony)
  • Total income: 775 S/month
  • Outgoings: MCC 45 S, basket (3.0× single) = 84 S, mortgage 1,257 S (at $1/S parity), discretionary 50 S
  • Net surplus: 775 - 1,436 = -661 S/month

Still negative. Even with the upgraded worker dividend (now 2.5× the previous level because Honda Inc isn't extracting), Joe can't afford his mortgage on UBI + dividend alone. His mortgage is sized as if he were earning external US wages — but in the SPICE model citizens don't earn external wages, they get UBI + dividends. So Joe runs his S balance down, defaults on housing, or relies on his spouse's income (if she's a remote worker, that helps a lot).

This is itself a finding. The default mortgage burden assumed in the founding generator is incompatible with the SPICE income model even with maximum worker dividend. Either citizens need still higher dividends (can the factory generate that much? probably not without scale increase), or the mortgage refinance lever has to be on, or UBI has to be much higher, or mortgage sizes assumed at founding need to be reduced. Worth raising as a configuration question.

7. Why it happens — the mechanism

  1. UBI mints unconditionally — 100 S/citizen/month, regardless of reserve health. At 3,900 citizens this is 390K S/month, 4.7M S/year. Adds to S supply mechanically.
  2. Reserve replenishment is bounded — exports + remote-worker income are fixed flows that don't grow with the S supply.
  3. Reserve drains are persistent — mortgages ($13M/yr at 10% scale) + citizen cashouts ($1-2M/yr) + imports ($5M/yr) consume most of the inflow.
  4. The Fisc defends cover ratio first, peg second — when reserve can't back target rate at 0.30 cover, rate compresses. Citizens bear the cost.
  5. No reserve-replenishment mechanism keyed to S supply — without LAT or S-tax, there's nothing to make the reserve grow as supply grows.

Note on Honda Inc. Earlier versions of this simulation modelled Honda Inc as extracting monthly dividends in USDC from its 60% Honda factory equity. That was wrong: a manufacturing subsidiary doesn't take dividends; it ships cars. The factory is a cost-center for Honda Inc, paid for via the export revenue (cars sold to Honda Inc). The current model has Honda Inc owning 60% on paper but extracting zero monthly dividend. The 87.5% of distributable surplus that previously went to Honda Inc as USDC now goes to Honda factory workers as S — making them the colony's wealthiest citizens but also generating cashouts of their own.

8. The same colony, mitigated

Run Transition again with two switches: LAT 5% and mortgage refinance to S.

Year-endFisc rateReserveS supplyBasket SReal PP
Y3 (M48)$1.44$22.4M$26.6M28.003.57
Y5 (M72)$1.78$18.3M$29.3M28.003.57
Y6 (M84)$1.70$15.3M$30.4M31.173.21
Y9 (M120)$1.29$14.5M$37.6M44.842.23

With two mitigations on:

  • Peg holds for Y0 through Y5 (vs. broken at Y3 in the unmitigated case)
  • Reserve peaks at $22M by Y3 — comfortably above the cover-ratio floor
  • By Y10 basket cost in S = 44.84 (vs. 84.46 unmitigated)
  • Real purchasing power loss = 37.5% (vs. 67%)

Roughly half the welfare loss with two switches. Neither mitigation fundamentally changes the design — they're plausible policy choices. LAT taxes the automated firms causing the deflation in the first place, redistributing some of that wealth back into reserve defence. Mortgage refinancing converts USD obligations (a $13M/year reserve drain) into S obligations that stay inside the colony.

This is what the simulation is for. The unmitigated Transition run shows the colony in trouble. The mitigated run shows what it takes to cope. Together they tell us the design can work, but only with deliberate reserve-replenishment + capital-retention mechanisms. The default no-LAT zero-mitigation baseline is not the recommended configuration — it's the experimental control.

Try the dashboard yourself. Start with Transition, no mitigations. Then turn on LAT, then mortgage refi, one at a time. Each lever's contribution becomes visible in the basket-cost and reserve charts.
