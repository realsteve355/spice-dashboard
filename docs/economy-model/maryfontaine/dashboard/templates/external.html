What's in the basket?

The basket is 4 categories totalling $980 USD per adult per month at colony founding (= 28 S at parity rate of $35/S). Weights match US Bureau of Labor Statistics CPI proportions. UBI of 100 S/month is therefore worth $3,500 in real terms at founding — a realistic basic-income equivalent. The simulator tracks how each category's USD price evolves under each scenario.

Category Weight USD at founding What it represents
Energy 28.6% $280 Gasoline, electricity, natural gas, heating fuel oil, household power
Food 32.1% $315 Groceries — food consumed at home. (Restaurants live under Services, since the value is mostly preparation + premises, not raw ingredients.)
Goods 17.9% $175 Manufactured durables + non-durables — clothing, electronics, furniture, household goods, vehicles, tools, appliances. The category most exposed to AI deflation in the scenario design.
Services 21.4% $210 Healthcare, education, transportation services, restaurants, professional services (legal, accounting), repair, recreation, internal rent. The category most exposed to wage-driven inflation in the scenario design.

What's deliberately abstracted away. The basket has no premium-vs-budget tiers, no quantity adjustments (citizens always consume the same basket-multiple regardless of price), no sub-category granularity (a healthcare cost spike has to be modelled by inflating Services as a whole), and no substitution (if energy doubles, citizens don't switch to cheaper alternatives — they just pay more or pay less). These simplifications are deliberate: they keep the model tractable and the 4-category weighting maps cleanly onto scenario design. A v2 could expand to 9 categories matching the CPI more closely.

Note on scale. Earlier model versions used a stylised $28 basket / $1/S parity rate. This was rescaled (May 2026) to realistic absolute dollar values: $980 basket / $35/S parity. The simulation dynamics are unchanged — peg-break timing, PP-loss percentages, and cover-ratio behaviour are scale-invariant under proportional rescaling. What changes is the labels you see (a $280 energy category, not $8) and the family stories making sense (mortgages a realistic fraction of consumption, not 50× a month's basket).

Energy USD price

Founding price: $8 / month for one citizen's share. Inflates under most scenarios as USD policy response keeps energy hot.

Food USD price

Founding price: $9. Mildly inflationary under most scenarios — food costs are sticky and weather-sensitive.

Hard goods USD price

Founding price: $5. The AI deflation engine bites here — automation collapses unit costs of manufactured goods.

Services USD price

Founding price: $6. Sticky-up under USD inflation regimes; only the AI Optimist scenario has services deflating.

Total basket cost in USD (the 4 categories combined)

Same set of goods that cost $28 at parity. This is what someone outside a SPICE colony pays. Compare with the basket-cost-in-S chart on the dashboard — that's what citizens inside the colony pay.

External purchasing power — what $100 USD buys (in baskets)

Outside the colony, $100 USD buys 100/$28 = 3.57 baskets at parity. Under The Transition, by Y10 it buys 100/$58 = 1.72 baskets — a 52% real loss for someone holding USD. The colony's job is to give citizens a steady-ground purchasing-power line that doesn't fall like this.

What the scenarios represent in the real world

AI Realist (default baseline)

The Dallas Fed (Aug 2025) productivity-effects view + Reinhart-Sbrancia (2015) financial-repression view, blended. AI deflation in goods at -3 to -5% / yr, services / food / energy nudging up at +2 to +3%. Mild stagflation tilt. Not a stress case — included as the sanity check that the engine produces sensible numbers under benign conditions.

AI Optimist

The McKinsey MGI (2023) + IMF WP/2025/076 view, taken at face value: productivity wins in every sector, AI deflation propagates from goods into services and even food. Reserve grows easily because imports are cheap and exports stay competitive. Risk in this scenario is over-deflation — basket cost in S could fall too fast for citizen comfort.

Stagflation

The classic policy-failure regime. USD instability dominates AI deflation. Energy +8 to +12% / yr, services and food sticky-up, only goods see modest deflation. Tests how the colony copes when the wider world's inflation regime resembles late-1970s OECD rather than the AI-deflation thesis. Reserve drains faster (imports more expensive in USD) but boundary outflows cost less in S terms.

AI + Healthcare crisis

AI Optimist trajectory for goods + energy + food, but services takes a +12 to +15% / yr shock for years 2-5 driven by healthcare costs (US healthcare grew at ~7% nominal pre-AI; assume the AI deflation in goods doesn't translate into healthcare savings as fast as elsewhere). Tests sector-specific stress. v1 lumps healthcare into services; a v2 5-category basket would let it be its own line.

The Transition (the credibility test)

Steve's view: AI deflation in tradable goods will be much steeper and more non-linear than typical economist baselines, because the fundamental cost structure of automated production isn't a Cobb-Douglas tweak — it's a different curve. Annual goods deflation accelerates from -8% in Y1 to -38% in Y5 before tapering. Simultaneously, monetary policy responds to deflation + debt with QE / yield-curve-control / financial repression, which keeps services and energy hot: services +8 → +18 → +2% over the decade, energy +10 → +22 → +2%. This is the scenario the simulation was built to stress-test.

In real-world terms: by Y5 a basket of hard goods that cost $5 in 2026 USD costs $1.24. Energy that cost $8 costs $17. Services that cost $6 cost $11. Anyone holding USD has watched their wealth get reshaped — not destroyed, but redistributed across categories in violent ways. Anyone with a 30-year mortgage at 5% pays the same nominal $1,400/month into a USD that's worth half what it was at origination. Anyone holding cash equivalents loses real value; anyone holding hard goods loses nominal value; anyone holding energy assets, services businesses, or scarcity stores wins big.

The Transition + Honda shock

The Transition baseline plus a discrete employer shock: Honda MaryFontaine's exports drop 50% in Q3 of Year 4 (automotive demand collapses as AI-driven design changes obsolete the existing assembly line), recovering linearly to 70% of original by Y6. Tests whether the colony survives a single-employer cashflow shock on top of structural deflation. The sort of thing that could plausibly hit Marysville given Honda's current concentration.

Why this matters

The colony isn't an island — it sits inside whichever of these external regimes happens to play out. The Fisc rate moves to anchor the basket at 28 S regardless of what the wider world does. That defence is what the dashboard charts measure. The contrast between the external purchasing-power chart on this page and the citizen purchasing-power chart on the dashboard is the load-bearing comparison. If the colony is doing its job, the inside line stays close to flat while the outside line falls. Where it doesn't, the design needs more reserve-replenishment leverage.
