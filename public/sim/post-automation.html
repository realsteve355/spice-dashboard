How a basic income pays for itself

When robots do the work, no one earns wages. So where does the money to live come from — and how does it not run out?

The idea, in plain terms

Robots do all the work, so no one earns a wage. The only way people can buy anything is a basic income — the community treasury (the Fisc) pays everyone a set amount each month.

It pays for itself by going round in a circle. People spend the basic income at the shops. The Fisc takes a cut of every sale — the Market Access Charge (MAC) — and that money goes back to the treasury to be paid out again next month.

The catch: the cut is only about a third of each sale, so one round of spending doesn't bring the whole basic income back. But the money is spent again and again through the month — the shops' takings get spent, and so on — and the Fisc takes its cut every time. After the money has gone round about three times, the cuts add up to the whole basic income, and the circle closes. The numbers below show it balancing.

Where the money goes — for every sale

Each sale is split: the Fisc takes the MAC, the robots cost a little to run, and the firm keeps the rest. The human shops pay no MAC — that money is a person's wage for real work.

Why the firms are happy to pay it

Even handing the Fisc a big cut of every sale, the automated firms keep a far higher margin than they do today — because they no longer pay any wages. That's the answer to "why would a company accept this?"
