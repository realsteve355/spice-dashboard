How far do prices actually fall?

Cheaper machines pull prices down — but the basic income they make necessary holds prices up. Where do they settle?

The question

The usual story is that AI and robots make things so cheaply that prices fall to a small fraction of today's — manufactured goods to about 10%, and so on. That assumes the entire saving from cheaper production turns into lower prices.

But there's a problem. When a machine replaces a worker, that worker stops earning — and stops being able to buy things. If wages disappear and prices simply drop, there's no one left to buy the cheap goods. So part of the saving has to become an income for the people who lost their jobs — a basic income, paid for by a charge that firms add to what they sell.

That charge sits inside every price. The more people who depend on the basic income, the bigger the charge has to be — so the less prices can actually fall. Cheaper production pulls prices down; the charge that funds the basic income holds them up. This page works out where they settle.

every price = what it costs to make  +  a charge that funds the basic income
