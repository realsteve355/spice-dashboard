The Time Dividend

When machines do everything, the gain isn't more money or cheaper goods. It's time.

The puzzle

Picture an economy where AI and robots do all the work, and no new jobs appear. The people they replace still need an income — otherwise no one can buy what the machines make, and the whole thing collapses. So we keep paying them: their old wage, now arriving as a basic income.

But then nothing in the money seems to change. The firms pay out the same amount; people receive the same; they spend the same. If the economy is identical, where is the benefit of all that automation? It looks like a wash — or worse, since someone still has to build and run the robots.

That is exactly the right question. The answer is that we are looking in the wrong place.

The money is conserved

Replace every worker with a robot, but have each firm keep paying the same wage bill — now as a charge that the treasury hands straight back out as a basic income. Trace the money and it goes round exactly as before: out as incomes, back as spending, out again. Same incomes, same spending, same firm revenue, same firm profit. The firms are no better and no worse off; people's living standard is unchanged. (The companion page, "It's the same economy", shows this balancing pound for pound.)

So the puzzle's premise is correct: in money terms, nothing changes. The mistake is to go looking for the benefit in the money.

The dividend of automation is time. the freed hours of everyone who used to work

Why "cheaper goods" is a mirage

It is tempting to say the goods got cheaper — the basket that cost $1,200 to make (mostly wages) now costs $300 (just energy and materials). But look at what was removed: the labour. And that removed labour is precisely the freed time.

"The goods are cheaper" and "people have free time" are not two benefits — they are one fact, stated twice. There is no extra abundance hiding behind the lower price tag. The cheapness is the freedom from work. Counting both is double-counting the only thing that changed.

Does it have a name?

Yes — an old and distinguished one, though no crisp modern label:

Karl Marx, the "Fragment on Machines" (Grundrisse, 1858). Once machines do the producing, he wrote, "the measure of wealth is then not any longer, in any way, labour time, but rather disposable time." The worker "steps to the side of the production process instead of being its chief actor." That is this principle, almost word for word.

John Maynard Keynes, "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren" (1930). He predicted productivity would shrink the work week toward fifteen hours and make the use of leisure the central problem — and coined "technological unemployment" in the same essay.

Marx's disposable time is the closest existing name. The exact form here — the money conserved, the only real dividend being freed time — we'll call the time dividend.

The one honest exception

There is a single thing that is not just free time: if the robots are genuinely more efficient with materials and energy than people were — less waste, running around the clock, or doing what humans simply couldn't — then the goods get cheaper in a non-labour way too, and that surplus could become more or better goods. That is real. But it is secondary, and it is an empirical question, not a logical necessity. Strip it out and the principle stands unbroken: the output is freed human time.

Why it matters

Almost every attempt to model a post-automation economy goes wrong in the same way: it invents a material windfall that isn't there — cheaper goods to fight over, fat corporate margins, an abundance of extra stuff. None of it exists. There is one dividend, it isn't money, and it's time.

So the policy question is not "how do we fund the abundance?" — there is no extra abundance to fund. It is simply: how do we share out the existing output, now that no one needs to work to make it? That is the entire job of the basic income — and it is why a model that claims only this is the one that survives examination. It claims exactly what is true, and nothing more.
