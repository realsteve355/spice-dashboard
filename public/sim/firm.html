A factory through automation

Following one ordinary factory's accounts as machines take over its work

What we are looking at

This page follows a single factory — a maker of everyday goods that employs 100 people today — as machines gradually take over its work. The aim is to see, in plain figures, what happens to the money as it does so. Five numbers in its accounts:

Sales (revenue)
the money the factory receives from selling what it makes.
Wages
what it pays the people who work there.
Materials & energy
what it pays for parts, raw materials and power.
Profit
what is left for the owners once wages, materials and the charge are paid.
The charge
a payment the factory makes that grows as it replaces people with machines. The money it raises is paid out as a basic income to people who no longer have a job. (Across the whole colony this charge is the Market Access Charge, or MAC.)

The question this page answers: as the factory automates and its goods get cheaper, can it still make a profit and pay a charge large enough to give the people it lets go an income to live on?

Two things you can change

The factory's accounts — today and once automated

Sales, less wages, less materials, less the charge, leaves profit. The wages it stops paying are far larger than the charge it takes on — so even with lower sales, the profit holds up.

The path, year by year

As the factory automates, the wages it pays fall away; the charge that funds a basic income for the people who left rises to take their place; and profit rises. The money that used to leave as wages now leaves partly as the charge and partly as profit — none of it disappears.
